A Champagne Glass, Not a Pyramid

If American wealth were a vessel, it would not be a wide pyramid with most at the bottom. It would be a champagne glass — a vast bowl of riches at the top resting on a needle-thin stem of everyone else.

Sidebar · Who is the middle class?

A group 40 times larger than the top 1% holds less of the country's wealth.

By the Pew Research Center's most-cited definition, a middle-class household of three earns between $56,600 and $169,800 a year (2022 dollars) — roughly two-thirds to double the U.S. median income. About 52% of American adults live in middle-income households by that measure.

By wealth, the picture is starker. The middle 40% of households — those between the 50th and 90th percentile, roughly 54 million households — together hold less of the nation's wealth than the top 1%.

Put another way: the typical middle-class family's wealth sits mostly in a single house and a retirement account. The typical top-1% household's wealth sits in business equity, stocks and bonds — assets that compound regardless of whether anyone shows up to work on Monday.

The Racial Wealth Gap

Same Country, Different Starting Lines

Wealth in America runs along racial lines that have persisted for generations — the result, in large part, of policy choices made over the last century.

Each row shows a racial group's share of total U.S. wealth alongside its share of households. The wider the gap, the less neutral the system.

The median white family has 6.3 times the wealth of the median Black family. Measured by average wealth, the gap in dollars has widened since 1983 — from roughly $320,000 to more than $1 million. The gap is the result of structural choices in housing, banking and inheritance law, not personal ones.
